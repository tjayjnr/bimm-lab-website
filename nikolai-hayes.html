    Nikolai Hayes

    Undergraduate Research Lead, B.S. Mechanical Engineering — New Mexico Institute of Mining and Technology

    Nikolai Hayes is a Mechanical Engineering student at New Mexico Institute of Mining and Technology and a student researcher on the NASA EPSCoR AQUASHIELD project. His work supports the project’s investigation of fluid transport behavior in additively manufactured structures, with a focus on capillary-driven flow through printed tube geometries. As part of the AQUASHIELD research team, Nikolai has been involved in developing, preparing, and testing capillary tube samples designed to evaluate how geometry, internal diameter, surface roughness, and material processing affect fluid rise behavior. His current work contributes to the broader goal of understanding how fluids can be managed within printed structures for future space-related shielding, thermal management, and fluid transport applications.

    Within the project, Nikolai’s responsibilities include designing and preparing capillary tube test samples, developing repeatable experimental procedures, organizing test matrices, and collecting data from capillary rise experiments. The tube designs include different internal channel geometries, such as circular, square, and triangular cross sections, as well as different hydraulic diameters and roughness conditions. He has helped refine the sample design process by considering factors such as tube height, channel size, external geometry, labeling, print reliability, and how to keep the experimental variables controlled. His work also includes preparing roughness and contact-angle test coupons to better understand how the printed material surface may influence capillary behavior.

    A significant part of Nikolai’s role has been procedure development. He has worked through practical challenges related to printing, washing, curing, cleaning, drying, labeling, and storing small capillary tube samples. This includes evaluating cleaning methods for resin-printed capillary channels, using isopropyl alcohol washing procedures, and learning to operate a vacuum desiccator to improve drying consistency before testing. He has also worked on measuring and documenting fluid properties, including the use of a Cannon-Fenske viscometer to support kinematic viscosity measurements for the fluids used in testing. These procedure-development steps are important because repeatability and sample preparation can strongly affect capillary rise results.

    Nikolai has also contributed to the experimental setup and data-collection workflow. The capillary rise tests involve recording fluid movement over time, tracking meniscus height, and comparing the behavior of different fluids and tube geometries. Based on pilot testing, the planned capillary rise test duration was shortened from twenty minutes to five minutes because observable rise for the selected fluids generally stopped within the first few minutes. This change makes testing more efficient while still capturing the meaningful portion of the rise behavior. Nikolai’s current testing progress includes completed initial testing for one smooth tube set, continued sample fabrication, and preparation for additional repeated trials and later analysis.

    In addition to hands-on laboratory work, Nikolai helps document the research through lab notes, meeting updates, slide presentations, and results summaries. He has prepared project slides, compiled lab notes, and summarized progress for meetings so that the work can be communicated clearly to advisors and collaborators. His role requires a combination of mechanical design, experimental testing, troubleshooting, and technical communication. Through AQUASHIELD, Nikolai is gaining experience in research methods, additive manufacturing, fluid mechanics, experimental uncertainty, and the practical challenges of producing reliable engineering data from a developing test system.
